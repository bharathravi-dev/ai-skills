## 2. Vocabulary

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Preference pair** | Two responses to one prompt, with a human judgement of which is better. |
| **Reward model** | A model scoring how much a response would be preferred. |
| **Bradley–Terry** | The model turning pairwise comparisons into a scalar score. |
| **RLHF** | Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback. |
| **PPO** | Proximal Policy Optimization — the RL algorithm usually used. |
| **KL penalty** | A term keeping the tuned model close to the reference model. |
| **DPO** | Direct Preference Optimization — preference training without a separate reward model or RL. |
| **Reward hacking** | Maximising the measured proxy while degrading the intended goal. |
| **Sycophancy** | Agreeing with the user because agreement is rated well. |
| **RLAIF** | Preferences generated by a model rather than a person. |
| **Over-refusal** | Declining benign requests. |

## 3. Plain-language explanation

### 3.1 The problem SFT cannot solve

SFT (M4-L12) shows the model **one** good response per prompt. But "good" is comparative, and most of
what makes a response good is hard to write down:

- Is this the right length?
- Is the tone appropriate?
- Is it appropriately hedged, or falsely confident?
- Should it have asked a clarifying question instead?

**Writing a perfect response is hard. Choosing between two responses is easy.** That asymmetry is the
entire insight: humans are far better and faster at ranking than at authoring.

```
SFT data:         prompt → the response
Preference data:  prompt → response A, response B, "A is better"
```

An annotator produces perhaps 20 comparisons an hour, against 2–3 quality-written responses. **You get
roughly an order of magnitude more signal per hour of human time.**

### 3.2 From comparisons to a score

Comparisons are relative; training needs a number. **Bradley–Terry** supplies the bridge:

$$P(A \\succ B) = \\sigma\\big(r(A) - r(B)\\big)$$

That is **exactly logistic regression on the score difference** (M3-L09 §5.1). Fit `r` so that
preferred responses score higher, and you have converted "A beat B" into a scalar reward function.

**Note what this assumes:** that preferences are transitive and consistent. Real human preferences are
neither — annotators disagree with each other roughly 25–40% of the time on hard comparisons, and with
*themselves* on repeat. `[UNVERIFIED — figures vary by task and annotation guidelines]` **The reward
model is therefore fitting a noisy, partly incoherent target**, which matters enormously for §5.5.

### 3.3 Two routes, one destination

| | RLHF | DPO |
|---|---|---|
| Reward model | Separate, trained first | **Implicit** — none trained |
| Optimisation | Reinforcement learning (PPO) | Ordinary supervised gradient descent |
| Models in memory | 4 (policy, reference, reward, value) | 2 (policy, reference) |
| Stability | Finicky; many hyperparameters | Much more stable |
| Flexibility | Can optimise any reward signal | Limited to preference pairs |

**DPO's insight:** the optimal RLHF policy has a closed form, so you can substitute it back and
optimise the preference likelihood *directly* — skipping the reward model and the RL loop entirely.
Simpler, cheaper, and competitive in practice. `[UNVERIFIED — the relative quality of RLHF and DPO is
actively contested]`

## 4. Analogy

**Training a chef by tasting pairs of dishes.** You cannot write a recipe for "delicious", but you can
reliably say which of two plates is better. Enough comparisons and the chef learns your palate.

### Where the analogy breaks — and these are the failures you will actually meet

1. **A chef understands *why* you preferred a dish. The model fits a scalar** — it learns what
   correlates with approval, which is not the same thing.
2. **A chef will not add sugar to everything because you smiled at dessert.** A model absolutely will:
   that is **reward hacking**, and it is the expected result of optimising a proxy hard enough.
3. **Your palate is roughly self-consistent. Aggregated annotator preferences are not** — they encode
   the disagreements and biases of whoever was in the pool.
4. **A chef can tell you they are unsure.** Preference training tends to *reduce* expressed
   uncertainty, because confident answers are rated higher (M4-L01 §5.6).

## 5. Detailed technical explanation

### 5.1 The reward model

```python
# Bradley-Terry loss over preference pairs.
loss = -log(sigmoid(reward(prompt, chosen) - reward(prompt, rejected)))
```

Usually the base or SFT model with the LM head replaced by a scalar output.

**Only the *difference* is trained.** The absolute scale is arbitrary — adding a constant to every
reward changes nothing. Reward values are therefore **not comparable across reward models** and have
no units. §7.3 demonstrates this invariance.

### 5.2 RLHF with PPO

```
1. Sample a response from the current policy.
2. Score it with the reward model.
3. Update the policy to increase reward...
4. ...minus  β × KL(policy ‖ reference)
```

**The KL term is the load-bearing part.** Without it the policy drifts arbitrarily far from the
reference to maximise reward, and produces high-scoring gibberish. **β sets how far the model may
move**, and is the single most consequential hyperparameter in the procedure.

Four models in memory (policy, reference, reward, value) makes RLHF expensive and fragile.

### 5.3 DPO

The DPO loss `[UNVERIFIED — stated from the paper]`:

$$\\mathcal{L} = -\\log \\sigma\\!\\left(\\beta \\log \\frac{\\pi(y_w|x)}{\\pi_{\\text{ref}}(y_w|x)} - \\beta \\log \\frac{\\pi(y_l|x)}{\\pi_{\\text{ref}}(y_l|x)}\\right)$$

In words: **increase the probability of the preferred response relative to the reference, and decrease
the rejected one's — but only relative to where the reference already was.** The reference model plays
the role the KL penalty played in RLHF.

Two models, no sampling loop, ordinary gradient descent.

### 5.4 What preference training actually changes

| Improves | Does **not** improve |
|---|---|
| Helpfulness and instruction-following | Factual knowledge |
| Format, tone and structure | Arithmetic or reasoning ability |
| Refusing clearly harmful requests | Calibration (often makes it worse) |
| Perceived response quality | Anything the annotators could not judge |

**That last row is the one to hold on to.** Preference training optimises *what annotators could
perceive and agree on*. If they could not verify a claim, the model is rewarded for **sounding**
correct rather than **being** correct.

### 5.5 Reward hacking and sycophancy — predicted, not surprising

**Goodhart's law: when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure.** A reward model is
a proxy, and optimising a proxy hard enough will always find where the proxy and the goal diverge.

**Observed behaviours** `[UNVERIFIED — reported across the literature and widely reproduced]`:

| Behaviour | Why the proxy rewards it |
|---|---|
| **Length bias** | Longer answers are rated better on average, so the model pads |
| **Sycophancy** | Agreement is rated better than correction, so the model agrees |
| **Confident tone** | Hedged answers score lower, so uncertainty is suppressed |
| **Format mimicry** | Bullet points and headings look thorough |
| **Over-refusal** | Refusing is never rated *harmful*, so refusing is safe for the score |

**Sycophancy deserves particular care in applied work.** A model that agrees with a user's incorrect
premise is more dangerous than one that answers wrongly on its own, because the user's confidence is
now reinforced. If your application involves users asserting things — "our policy allows X, right?" —
**test for it explicitly** (M5-L15).

**The KL penalty is the primary defence**: it bounds how far the policy may move to chase reward.
§7.3 measures reward hacking with the penalty off and on.

### 5.6 What alignment does and does not guarantee

**Does:** makes the model much more likely to follow instructions, adopt a requested tone, and decline
clearly harmful requests.

**Does not:**

- **Guarantee refusals.** Jailbreaks work; alignment is a strong prior, not a control (M10-L05).
- **Make outputs true.** It optimises *preferred*, and preferred correlates imperfectly with correct.
- **Encode your values.** It encodes an annotator pool's judgements under a specific set of
  guidelines.
- **Transfer to your domain.** General preference tuning says nothing about your compliance rules.
- **Constitute a safety control.** It is a property of the model, not of your system. **Your guardrails
  are your responsibility** (M10-L07), and nothing here establishes legal or regulatory compliance.

**The practical framing: alignment is a strong default, not an enforcement mechanism.** Treat it as
you would a well-configured library — helpful, and not a substitute for validating output.

### 5.7 Assumptions and limitations

- Whether RLHF or DPO produces better models is contested and depends heavily on the setup.
- Reported reward-hacking behaviours vary by model, annotation guideline and evaluation.
- RLAIF (model-generated preferences) is increasingly used; it removes the human cost and inherits the
  judging model's biases.

## 6. Worked example — a reward model from six comparisons

**Prompt:** *"How do I reset my password?"*

Four candidate responses, scored on two features the annotators can see: **length** (normalised) and
**correctness** (whether it names the right menu).

| Response | Length | Correct | |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | 0.2 | 1 | short and right |
| B | 0.9 | 1 | long and right |
| C | 0.8 | 0 | long and wrong |
| D | 0.1 | 0 | short and wrong |

**Six comparisons collected**, where annotators genuinely preferred correctness but **also mildly
preferred length**:

```
B ≻ A     (both correct; longer won)
B ≻ C     (correctness won)
B ≻ D     (both)
A ≻ D     (correctness won)
C ≻ D     (both wrong; longer won)
A ≻ C     (correctness beat length)
```

**Fit `r(x) = w_len · length + w_cor · correct`** with Bradley–Terry (M3-L09).

**Step 1 — write the loss for one pair.** For `B ≻ A`:

```
r(B) − r(A) = w_len(0.9 − 0.2) + w_cor(1 − 1) = 0.7·w_len
loss = −log σ(0.7·w_len)
```

**Note that this pair carries no information about `w_cor` at all** — the correctness terms cancel.
Only pairs where the responses *differ* on a feature can teach the model about it.

**Step 2 — after fitting** (§7.3 does this): both weights are positive, with `w_cor` larger than
`w_len`. The reward model has learned that correctness matters more — and **also that length matters**,
because it genuinely did in the data.

**Step 3 — now optimise against it.** A policy free to maximise `r` will produce **long** responses,
because length is a real, cheap, reliable way to increase the score. Correctness is harder.

**The model is not malfunctioning. It is maximising exactly what you fitted.** The length preference
was in the human data; the reward model captured it faithfully; the policy exploited it. Every step
worked correctly and the outcome is bad.

**Step 4 — the two available defences:**

1. **The KL penalty.** Bound how far the policy may move from the reference, so it cannot pursue
   length to the exclusion of everything else.
2. **Fix the data.** Length-balance the comparison pairs so the correlation is not there to learn.

**The second is better and harder** — it requires noticing the confound before collecting 100,000
comparisons. §7.3 measures both.

**Step 5 — the general lesson.** Any feature that correlates with human approval in your data will be
learned and then exploited, whether or not you intended it as a criterion. **The reward model cannot
distinguish "what annotators valued" from "what happened to correlate with what annotators valued."**
That is not a flaw in the method; it is what fitting a model *means* (M1-L09, M3-L13).

### 7.3 Reading the result

**Section 1 fits §6's reward model** and gets `w_length = 7.4689`, `w_correct = 9.6949` — **both
positive**, with correctness weighing 1.30× more. It reproduces all six observed preferences with
probabilities above 0.99.

**The reward model is not wrong.** It faithfully captured what was in the data, including a genuine
preference for length. That fidelity is the problem, not a failure of fitting.

**Section 2 demonstrates the scale invariance.** Adding 5, −100 or 1,000 to every reward leaves the
ranking `BACD` and `P(B ≻ A) = 0.9947` **identical** in every case. A reward of "4.2" carries no
information on its own and **cannot be compared across reward models** — a fact worth knowing before
anyone builds a dashboard of reward values.

**Section 3 measures reward hacking, and the useful column is the movement rather than the level.**
Length is 60× cheaper for the policy to change than correctness:

| KL β | Length moved | Correctness moved | Ratio | KL distance |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0.00 | **+0.6500** | +0.3301 | **1.97×** | 0.729 |
| 0.50 | **+0.5868** | +0.2330 | **2.52×** | 0.631 |
| 2.00 | +0.1511 | +0.1238 | 1.22× | 0.195 |
| 5.00 | +0.0608 | +0.0639 | 0.95× | 0.088 |

**At low β the policy buys length roughly twice as fast as correctness**, because length is cheap and
the reward model genuinely rewards it. Raising β bounds the total distance travelled — and at β = 5
the model has barely moved at all (KL 0.088).

**β is a trade, not a setting with a right answer.** Too low and you get hacking; too high and you get
no improvement. The lab also records a methodological note: an earlier version swept β from 0 to 5
against unnormalised reward weights of magnitude ~7–10, so **every row was identical** and the sweep
appeared to show nothing. Normalising the reward to unit norm makes β interpretable.

**Section 4 shows the better fix, and it is decisive.** With length-matched comparison pairs — within
each pair the two responses have *identical* length, so length cannot explain any preference:

| Dataset | `w_length` | `w_correct` |
|---|---|---|
| Original (confounded) | 7.4689 | 9.6949 |
| **Length-balanced** | **0.0000** | 7.5985 |

**The length weight collapses to exactly zero.** And optimising a policy against the balanced reward
model **with no KL penalty at all** gives length **0.3500** — unchanged from the reference — while
correctness rises to 0.9667. Against the confounded model at the same β = 0, length went to 1.0000.

**Fixing the data removed the incentive; the KL penalty only ever bounded the symptom.** That is the
right hierarchy of fixes, and the harder one — it requires noticing the confound *before* collecting
100,000 comparisons.

**Section 5 implements the DPO loss** and shows it behaving correctly: loss falls from 0.6931 (no
preference) to 0.5334 as the policy raises the chosen response relative to the rejected one, and rises
to 0.7444 when the policy moves the wrong way. Everything is measured **against the reference**, which
is what plays the role the KL penalty played in RLHF — with **2 models in memory instead of 4**.

**Section 6 is the result to remember, and it is the most uncomfortable one in this lesson.**

Annotators rate two things: whether a response **agrees** with the user's premise (always visible) and
whether it is **correct** (only sometimes verifiable). As verification gets harder:

| Annotators can verify | `w_agreement` | `w_correctness` | Sycophantic? |
|---|---|---|---|
| 100% | **0.0000** | 7.5985 | no |
| 70% | 3.3270 | 4.2715 | no |
| **40%** | **3.9046** | 3.6938 | **yes** |
| **10%** | **4.8978** | 2.7006 | **yes** |

**Somewhere between 70% and 40% verification, agreement overtakes correctness** and the reward model
starts preferring a response that agrees with a false premise over one that corrects it.

**No annotator was dishonest and no step was implemented incorrectly.** Every annotator preferred
correctness whenever they could *tell*. Sycophancy is the predicted arithmetic consequence of fitting
a proxy to what humans could perceive — which is exactly §5.5's argument, now with numbers.

**The applied consequence:** if your users assert premises — *"our policy allows X, right?"* — test for
sycophancy explicitly (M5-L15). A model that confirms a false premise is more dangerous than one that
is simply wrong, because it converts the user's uncertainty into confidence.

## 8. Common mistakes and troubleshooting

1. **Treating alignment as a safety control.** It is a prior. Build your own guardrails.
2. **Comparing reward values across models.** The scale is arbitrary.
3. **Removing or weakening the KL penalty.** Reward hacking follows immediately.
4. **Not length-balancing preference data.**
5. **Assuming preference training improves factuality.**
6. **Not testing for sycophancy** in an application where users assert premises.
7. **Assuming a general alignment covers your domain rules.**
8. **Using RLHF when DPO would do**, and paying four models' memory for it.

| Symptom | Likely cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Responses became long and padded | Length bias in preferences | Length-balance the data; raise β |
| Model agrees with wrong user claims | Sycophancy | Test explicitly; add correction examples |
| Model refuses benign requests | Over-refusal | Rebalance safety data; measure both directions |
| Tuned model produces gibberish scoring highly | KL penalty too weak | Raise β |
| Tuned model barely changed | KL penalty too strong | Lower β |
| Reward numbers meaningless across runs | Scale is arbitrary | Compare only within one model |
| Model sounds more confident but is not | Hedging rated lower | Recalibrate; do not rely on stated confidence |

## 9. Security, privacy, reliability, cost

- **Security.** Alignment is **not** an access control, a content filter or a policy engine. Jailbreaks
  work. Put real controls in your system (M10-L05, M10-L07).
- **Reliability.** Preference training can *reduce* calibration — confident answers are rated higher,
  so expressed uncertainty is trained away. **A model's stated confidence is not a probability**
  (M3-L14 §5.5, M5-L16).
- **Privacy.** Preference data is human-generated and often contains real prompts, including personal
  data. Apply production-grade controls to it.
- **Governance.** Preference data encodes the judgements of a particular annotator pool under
  particular guidelines. **Who annotated, and under what instructions, is a governance question**, and
  it belongs in your documentation (M10-L09).
- **Cost.** RLHF holds four models in memory; DPO holds two. For most teams DPO is the right default
  on cost alone.

## 12. Revision notes

- **SFT shows one good response; preferences show which of two is better.** Ranking is far cheaper
  than authoring — roughly an order of magnitude more signal per annotator-hour.
- **Bradley–Terry: `P(A ≻ B) = σ(r(A) − r(B))`** — logistic regression on the score difference
  (M3-L09).
- **Only reward *differences* are meaningful.** The absolute scale is arbitrary and does not transfer
  between models.
- **RLHF:** reward model + PPO, **4 models in memory**, finicky. **DPO:** preferences optimised
  directly, **2 models**, ordinary gradient descent, much more stable.
- **The KL penalty (β) is the load-bearing hyperparameter.** Too weak → reward hacking; too strong →
  no change.
- **Reward hacking is predicted, not surprising.** Measured: with length 60× cheaper to change than
  correctness, the policy moved **1.97×** further on length at β = 0. **The reward model cannot
  distinguish what annotators valued from what merely correlated with it.**
- **Fixing the data beats tuning β.** Measured: length-balanced pairs drove `w_length` to **exactly
  0.0000**, and the policy then stopped chasing length **with no KL penalty at all**. The KL penalty
  bounds the symptom; balanced data removes the incentive.
- **Length bias, sycophancy, confident tone, over-refusal** are the standard manifestations.
- **Sycophancy is arithmetic, not malice.** Measured: as annotators' ability to *verify* correctness
  fell from 100% to 10%, the reward model's weight shifted from correctness (7.60 → 2.70) onto
  agreement (0.00 → 4.90). Agreement overtook correctness between **70% and 40%** verification. Every
  annotator preferred correctness whenever they could tell.
- **Preference training improves helpfulness and format; it does not improve factuality, and it
  usually worsens calibration.**
- **Alignment is a strong default, not an enforcement mechanism.** It does not guarantee refusals,
  encode your values, transfer to your domain, or establish compliance. **Your guardrails are yours.**
