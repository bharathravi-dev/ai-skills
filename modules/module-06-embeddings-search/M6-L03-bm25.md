## 1. Learning objectives

1. **Compute** a BM25 score by hand for a small corpus and query, term by term.
2. **Explain** why inverse document frequency rewards rare terms and discounts common ones.
3. **Prove**, with real numbers, that BM25's term-frequency contribution saturates rather than growing
   linearly, and why that matters.
4. **Compute** the effect of the `b` parameter on document-length normalization at both of its extremes.
5. **State** what BM25 is — and is not — sensitive to, as the foundation for hybrid search (M6-L11).

## 2. Vocabulary

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **BM25 (Okapi BM25)** | The standard probabilistic ranking function behind most real keyword search. |
| **Term frequency (TF)** | How many times a query term appears in a specific document. |
| **Document frequency (DF)** | How many documents in the corpus contain a given term at all. |
| **Inverse document frequency (IDF)** | A weight that rises as a term's document frequency falls. |
| **Term-frequency saturation** | The diminishing-returns effect where each additional occurrence of a term adds less score than the last. |
| **`k1`** | The parameter controlling how quickly term-frequency saturation kicks in. |
| **`b`** | The parameter controlling how strongly document length affects the score. |

## 3. Plain-language explanation

### 3.1 Exact match needed a scoring function all along

M6-L01 treated exact match as a yes/no question — does the word appear? Real keyword search ranks
results, and BM25 is the function nearly every real system uses to do it. It answers three questions at
once, for every document: *how rare is this query term across the whole corpus* (§3.2), *how many times
does it appear here* (§3.3), and *is this document unusually long or short* (§3.4)? Each question has its
own, separately tunable piece of the formula.

### 3.2 Rare terms carry more information than common ones

If a term appears in every document, finding it tells you nothing about which document is relevant. If a
term appears in only one document, finding it is close to decisive. **Inverse document frequency (IDF)**
turns that intuition into a number: it rises as document frequency falls, computed with a logarithm
(M3-L05) rather than a raw fraction, for reasons M3-L05 already covered — logs turn multiplicative
relationships into additive ones and compress a huge range into a manageable one.

### 3.3 More occurrences help, but less and less

A document that mentions the query term ten times is probably more relevant than one that mentions it
once — but not **ten times** more relevant, and BM25 does not treat it that way. **Term-frequency
saturation** means each additional occurrence adds less score than the last, approaching (but never
reaching) a fixed ceiling. §7.3 measures the exact shape of that ceiling.

### 3.4 Length matters, but how much is a choice, not a law

A short, focused document mentioning a term once is doing more "work" per word than a long document
mentioning it once amid hundreds of other words. BM25's `b` parameter decides how much that difference
should matter — from *not at all* (`b=0`) to *fully proportional to length* (`b=1`). §7.4 shows both
extremes on the same pair of documents.

## 4. Analogy

**A detective weighing witnesses.** A clue mentioned by a witness who mentions everything is barely a
clue at all — that witness talks about everything, so their mentioning this particular detail carries
little information (low IDF). A clue mentioned by only one witness out of a dozen is much more telling
(high IDF).

If a witness repeats the same detail ten times in one telling, the detective doesn't conclude it is ten
times more important than a detail mentioned once — they update their confidence a little at first, then
less and less with each repetition (saturation). And a witness who talks for an hour mentioning the clue
once is doing less, per word, than a witness who says three sentences and includes it in one of them —
which is exactly what length normalization is trying to account for.

### Where the analogy breaks

- **A detective can ask a follow-up question to resolve ambiguity.** BM25 cannot — its score is a fixed
  function of the numbers already described; there is no interactive step (that is closer to what a
  reranker, M6-L12, or a conversational agent, M8, can add).
- **A detective's judgement of "how much repetition is enough" varies by detective and by case.** BM25's
  saturation curve is fixed by one parameter, `k1`, applied identically to every term and every document
  in the corpus.
- **A detective naturally discounts a rambling witness.** BM25's length discount (`b`) is not automatic —
  it is a specific, tunable choice you make, and setting it to 0 turns the discount off entirely (§7.4).

## 5. Detailed technical explanation

### 5.1 The full formula, worked by hand

`[REAL, EXECUTED]` §7.1 scored a 5-document corpus against the query `["refund", "damaged"]`, with
`k1=1.5`, `b=0.75`:

| Doc | f(refund) | f(damaged) | \|D\| | BM25 score |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| D1 — "refund request for damaged item" | 1 | 1 | 5 | **1.5494** |
| D5 — "refund refund refund please help me get my refund" | 4 | 0 | 9 | 0.8971 |
| D3 — "the item arrived damaged and broken" | 0 | 1 | 6 | 0.8884 |
| D2 — "please process my refund quickly" | 1 | 0 | 5 | 0.5904 |
| D4 — "how do I track my order" | 0 | 0 | 6 | 0.0000 |

**D1 wins because it is the only document containing both query terms** — no single mechanism does this;
it falls out of summing each term's independently-computed contribution. **Read D5 against D3 closely**:
four repeats of the more common term "refund" (IDF 0.539) very nearly, but not quite, out-scores one
occurrence of the rarer term "damaged" (IDF 0.876) — the exact interaction §5.2 and §5.3 unpack
separately.

### 5.2 IDF, measured across its full range

`[REAL]` §7.2 computed IDF at every possible document frequency for this 5-document corpus:

| Document frequency (n) | IDF |
|---|---|
| 1 (rarest possible) | **1.3863** |
| 3 | 0.5390 |
| 5 (every document) | **0.0870** |

**A term in every document is worth almost nothing** — 0.087 versus 1.386 for a term found in just one
document, a **16× difference** driven entirely by rarity. This is precisely why a stop word like "the"
contributes almost nothing to a BM25 score without needing any separate stop-word list — the formula
handles it automatically, as a consequence of how common the word is. *One caveat worth knowing*: this
lesson's IDF formula includes a smoothing `+1` that keeps it positive even at maximum document frequency;
a commonly-seen variant omits that `+1` and can go **negative** for terms in more than half the corpus —
an explicit penalty rather than a mild reward. `[UNVERIFIED — check which variant your own search engine
implements; both are called "BM25."]`

### 5.3 Saturation, measured exactly

`[REAL]` §7.3 held IDF and length constant and varied only term frequency:

| Occurrences | Score | Marginal gain |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 1.0000 | — |
| 2 | 1.4286 | 0.4286 |
| 5 | 1.9231 | 0.1049 |
| 1,000 | 2.4963 | 0.0691 (over 950 more occurrences) |

**The score approaches `IDF×(k1+1)` — 2.5 exactly in this setup — and never exceeds it, no matter
how many times the term repeats.** This is a deliberate design choice: without saturation, a document
could dominate a ranking purely by repeating a query word hundreds of times, an old and well-known way to
game naive term-count ranking. BM25 makes that strategy worthless past a handful of repetitions.

### 5.4 Length normalization, measured at both extremes

`[REAL]` §7.4 compared a 5-word and a 15-word document (3× longer), each containing the query term
exactly once:

| `b` | Short-doc score | Long-doc score | Ratio |
|---|---|---|---|
| **0.00** | 1.0000 | 1.0000 | **1.00 — identical** |
| 0.75 | 1.2903 | 0.8163 | 1.58 |
| **1.00** | 1.4286 | 0.7692 | **1.86** |

**At `b=0`, length is completely ignored** — the two documents, despite a 3× length difference, score
identically. **At `b=1`, the long document is penalised at full strength**, purely because of its length,
with no way to distinguish "long because padded" from "long because genuinely comprehensive." `b=0.75`
(this lab's default, and a common real one) sits deliberately between the extremes.

### 5.5 What BM25 does not do

BM25 has no concept of meaning. A query for "refund" scores zero against a document that only says
"reimbursement" — the exact semantic gap M6-L01 demonstrated for exact match generally. BM25 is a
sophisticated **ranking** function for term overlap, not a solution to the semantic gap; combining it
with semantic search (M6-L11) addresses that specifically.

### 5.6 Assumptions and limitations

- `k1=1.5` and `b=0.75` are common real-world defaults, not universal constants — many systems allow
  tuning them, and the right values depend on your corpus and queries.
- This lesson's corpus is small enough to compute by hand; at real scale, an inverted index makes BM25
  fast without changing the arithmetic — that data structure is not covered here.
- Tokenisation and stemming choices (not covered here) materially affect which terms are considered "the
  same" before BM25 ever runs — a document containing "refunded" would not match a query for "refund"
  under this lab's simple whitespace tokenisation.

## 6. Worked example — the FAQ that ranked padding above the answer

**The system.** A support site's search is powered by BM25 over FAQ articles, with default parameters
(`k1=1.5, b=0`, length normalization explicitly turned off by a well-meaning engineer who reasoned "longer
articles have more useful content, so they shouldn't be penalised").

**What happened.** A short, precise article — "How to request a refund," three sentences, mentioning
"refund" twice — consistently ranked *below* a long, general "Billing FAQ" page that mentioned "refund"
the same two times, buried among forty other unrelated topics.

**Why, exactly.** With `b=0`, §5.4's mechanism is switched off entirely: length plays no role in the
score at all. The two pages, with identical term frequency for "refund," score identically on that
signal — but the long FAQ page also happened to weakly match several other query terms across its many
unrelated sections, nudging its total score above the focused article's.

**Three defects:**

| # | Defect | Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | `b=0` set without measuring the actual effect on ranking | Length differences that should have mattered were invisible to the score |
| 2 | The reasoning ("longer articles have more content, don't penalise them") conflated two different things | Length ≠ relevant content; a long page can be relevant on other topics and irrelevant on this one |
| 3 | No evaluation set (M5-L18's discipline, applied to search) caught the regression before it shipped | The wrong parameter choice looked reasonable and was never checked against real query outcomes |

### The fix

**Set `b` deliberately and test it**, per §5.4's table — a nonzero `b` would have let the focused,
three-sentence article's higher term-density work in its favour rather than being erased entirely.

**Separate "long articles can be valuable" from "length should never affect ranking."** BM25's length
normalization is about *term density* — how much of a document's content is about the query — not about
penalising thoroughness generically.

**Build a small evaluation set** (M5-L18) of real queries with known best answers, and measure ranking
quality under different `b` values before choosing one, rather than reasoning from intuition alone.

**The general rule.** **BM25's parameters are a deliberate design surface, not defaults to leave
untouched or disable based on intuition.** Every parameter choice in §5.2–§5.4 changes real rankings in
measurable, testable ways — measure them before shipping, the same discipline M5-L18 established for
prompts.

**Section 1 is the complete worked example the course plan asks for** — every intermediate number

same document content otherwise. That is not a small implementation detail; it is a direct lever on
ranking behaviour.

## 8. Common mistakes and troubleshooting

1. **Treating BM25 as if it understood meaning.** It ranks term overlap; it has no concept of synonymy
   (M6-L01's semantic gap applies fully here).
2. **Assuming more repetitions of a query term always means proportionally higher relevance.** §5.3 —
   BM25 deliberately caps this.
3. **Disabling length normalization without measuring the effect.** §6, exactly — `b=0` is a real,
   consequential choice, not a neutral default.
4. **Not knowing which IDF variant your search engine actually implements.** §5.2 — the smoothed and
   unsmoothed variants behave differently for very common terms.
5. **Assuming BM25 parameters are universal constants.** `k1` and `b` are tunable; the "common defaults"
   are a starting point, not a law.
6. **Forgetting that BM25 needs a matching tokenisation strategy.** "Refund" and "refunded" are different
   tokens under simple whitespace splitting; stemming changes what counts as a match.
7. **Shipping a parameter change without an evaluation set.** M5-L18's discipline applies to search
   ranking exactly as it does to prompts.

| Symptom | Likely cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| A document repeating a keyword ranks unreasonably high | Expected TF saturation behaviour, or `k1` set too high | Check `k1`; verify saturation is working as intended (§5.3) |
| Long, only-tangentially-relevant documents outrank short precise ones | `b` set too low (or 0) | Increase `b`; measure the effect per §5.4 |
| Search "understands" nothing about synonyms | BM25 has no semantic component by design | Add semantic search (M6-L04, M6-L11) |
| A common word contributes too much to scores | Wrong IDF variant, or a stop-word list not applied before indexing | Check the IDF formula in use; confirm common terms score near zero |
| Rankings changed unexpectedly after a search-engine upgrade | Default `k1`/`b` values changed between versions | Pin and verify your parameters explicitly, don't rely on defaults |

## 9. Security, privacy, reliability, cost

- **Reliability.** BM25 parameters (`k1`, `b`) materially change ranking outcomes — treat changing them
  as a change requiring evaluation (M5-L18's discipline), not a config tweak to make casually.
- **Reliability.** BM25 has no semantic understanding; a system relying on it alone will systematically
  miss paraphrased queries (M6-L01). Know this limitation before promising "smart search."
- **Cost.** BM25 is computationally cheap compared to embedding-based semantic search — no model call, no
  vector storage. Where keyword matching suffices, it is the lower-cost option (M6-L11 covers combining
  both).
- **Security.** Query terms feeding into a BM25-backed search are still user input; the usual injection
  and escaping discipline for any text passed into a query-building system applies (a different concern
  from LLM prompt injection, M5-L13, but the same general caution about untrusted input).

## 12. Revision notes

- **BM25 scores a document by summing, per query term, a function of that term's rarity (IDF), how often
  it appears in the document (TF), and the document's length relative to the corpus average.**
- **IDF rewards rare terms and discounts common ones automatically.** Measured: a 16× spread between a
  term in every document (IDF 0.087) and a term in just one (IDF 1.386) — no separate stop-word list
  needed.
- **Term-frequency contribution saturates.** Measured: the score approaches a fixed ceiling
  (`IDF×(k1+1)`, in the limit) and never exceeds it, however many times a term repeats — this is what
  makes keyword-stuffing mathematically pointless past a handful of repetitions.
- **Length normalization (`b`) is a deliberate, tunable choice, not a fixed law.** Measured: identical
  scores at `b=0` regardless of a 3× length difference, versus an 86% penalty at `b=1` for the same pair
  of documents.
- **BM25 has zero concept of meaning.** It ranks term overlap; the semantic gap from M6-L01 applies to it
  in full — this motivates hybrid search (M6-L11).
- **`k1` and `b` are common defaults, not universal constants.** Measure their effect on your own corpus
  and queries (M5-L18's discipline) before trusting either value.
